Two companies took nearly half the money

In March, OpenAI raised $122 billion. In May, Anthropic raised $65 billion. Together that is $217 billion — 43 cents of every venture dollar invested worldwide in the first half of the year. The chart below shows the split. Everything in this article lives inside the smaller slice: $27 billion reported by 1,635 smaller companies in public SEC filings.

Sources: company announcements via WSJ/AI trade press (OpenAI Mar, Anthropic May 28); H1 venture total $510B per Crunchbase summaries. Our count: clean US-operating Reg D cut.

April was the busiest month

Companies filed 586 times in April, dipped to 481 in May, and bounced back to 527 in June. April is when winter board meetings turn into signed checks. June had louder competition for attention: the World Cup kicked off on June 11 across 16 American, Mexican, and Canadian cities, and world leaders met in Évian, France, from June 15 to 17 to talk about Ukraine, the Middle East, and how fast to deploy AI.

Most raises were small. Most dollars were huge.

Half of all companies (831 of 1,635) raised less than $3 million — the classic early bet. But just 198 companies, each raising over $25 million, collected $21 billion, or 77 cents of every dollar. That split is normal in startups. It was just more extreme this spring.

New York filed more than San Francisco

New York companies filed 114 times, nearly double San Francisco's 58. Austin had 30, Miami 19. But read the map carefully: 71 percent of these companies are legally incorporated in Delaware, and 44 list Delaware addresses that are really lawyers' offices. Talent lives in New York and California. Paperwork lives in Wilmington.

California led with $7.87 billion across 289 companies. New York followed with $4 billion across 151 companies. Texas, North Carolina, Massachusetts, and Florida came next, in that order.

The AI money went to shovels

While the two giants bought computing power and distribution, investors poured money into the companies that sell to the builders. Baseten, which rents out AI computing, closed $1.5 billion in June. Supabase, the database behind the "vibe-coding" craze, raised $500 million. Legal AI, general-purpose agents, and AI cloud companies all raised hundreds of millions. The pattern is plain: when the platforms boom, the tools boom first.

Amounts are shown in millions of dollars. Some bars show one part of a larger funding round. Baseten, for example, later confirmed a $1.5 billion round in June.

Investors also backed ships, rockets, magnets, and digital money

Outside AI, four themes stand out. Autonomy: Saronic's $1 billion filing (part of a $1.75 billion round) funds robot ships for the Navy, and Shield AI's $592 million funds autonomy software — real defense money, far from the famous names.

Space: Cowboy ($305M), Stoke ($275M), and Cesium ($271M) are building rockets and satellite gear in the wake of the SpaceX–xAI merger. Atoms: Vulcan Elements raised $431 million in North Carolina to make rare-earth magnets at home. Coins: after the GENIUS Act gave stablecoins a federal license in late May, crypto exchange Kraken reported $354 million and Circle $222 million — while Circle's dollar coin grew to $73 billion in circulation.

How they raised it

Almost everything was straight equity: 956 deals, $18.3 billion. Debt was rarer but bigger per deal (110 deals, $3.5 billion). The much-discussed SAFE — the one-page startup IOU — showed up in only about 77 filings, mostly the smallest ones. And remarkably, 340 of these companies were founded this same year. In 2026, you can go from incorporation to financing in months.

Six companies are selling tools to the AI builders

When computing demand outruns supply, the toolmakers raise money first. These six did.

Baseten

This San Francisco company rents out AI computing to other startups. It reported $1.1 billion in these filings, part of a $1.5 billion round it closed in June that valued it at $13 billion. It was its fourth fundraise in 18 months.

Based in San Francisco. Sells AI computing.

Superhuman

This is Grammarly under a new name. The writing assistant reported just over $1 billion in April, the largest single writing-software financing in the dataset. It was founded in 2009 and is based in San Francisco.

Based in San Francisco. Makes writing software.

Supabase

This company makes the database behind the "vibe-coding" trend, where people describe apps in plain words. It reported $440 million, part of a $500 million round in June that valued it at $10.5 billion. Its backers include Stripe and Salesforce.

Incorporated in Delaware. Sells databases for app builders.

Generalist

This robotics company builds brains, not bodies: AI models that let different robots learn new tasks from seconds of video. Founded by former Google DeepMind and Boston Dynamics engineers, it reported $364 million, part of a $400 million round in June that valued it at $2 billion. Its backers include Nvidia.

Based in California. Builds robot software.

Harvey

This company makes AI assistants for lawyers. It reported $200 million from California, a sign that AI money is reaching one profession at a time, starting with law.

Based in California. Makes legal software.

SharonAI

This Australian cloud company rents out AI computing across Asia and the Pacific. It reported $350 million, part of a $1.6 billion financing it closed in June to buy as many as 40,000 Nvidia chips with Oaktree Capital. It trades on the Nasdaq.

Filed from New York. Rents out AI computing.

Five bets on the sea and the sky

The quarter's most physical money went to ships that sail themselves and rockets built for data centers.

Saronic

This Austin company builds crewless warships. It reported just over $1 billion, part of a $1.75 billion round in March that valued it at $9.25 billion. The Navy has already ordered $392 million of its small Corsair vessels, and it is scouting sites for a giant new shipyard in Texas.

Based in Austin. Builds autonomous ships.

Shield AI

This company writes the piloting software for military drones. It reported $592 million from California, alongside reports of a round above $2 billion that valued it near $13 billion. Sea and air autonomy were funded within days of each other.

Based in California. Writes drone software.

Cowboy Space

Robinhood co-founder Baiju Bhatt renamed his space-solar startup Cowboy in May and raised $275 million at a $2 billion valuation to build rockets whose upper stages double as orbital data centers. Each would carry about 800 AI chips powered by sunlight. Its first launch is planned for 2028.

Based in San Carlos, California. Builds rockets and orbital data centers.

Stoke Space

This Washington company is building Nova, a fully reusable rocket meant to fly like an airplane. It reported $275 million, part of a round that reached $860 million in February. It has now raised $1.34 billion and is preparing its Cape Canaveral launch site.

Based in Kent, Washington. Builds reusable rockets.

CesiumAstro

This Austin company builds communications gear for satellites. It reported $271 million, which makes Austin the quarter's second space cluster after the California coast.

Based in Austin. Builds satellite communications.

Ramp

Based in New York. Sells business spending software.

Payward (Kraken)

The issuer of the USDC digital dollar reported $222 million from New York. In the same quarter its coin reached $73 billion in circulation and it won approval for a federal trust bank. Nearly all of its revenue still comes from interest on reserves.

Based in New York. Issues the USDC digital dollar.

Mercury

This startup bank for startups reported $200 million from California. When thousands of newly funded companies need accounts the same quarter, their bank raises too.

Based in California. Provides startup banking.

SonoThera

Based in North Carolina. Makes rare-earth magnets.

Based in Texas. Builds hypersonic aircraft.

Sixteen nearly identical $5 million filings arrived from Edmond, Oklahoma, one per series of a heat-safety business. Filed as summer approached and World Cup stadiums filled, it reads as climate adaptation sold the way franchises are sold.

Based in Edmond, Oklahoma. Works on heat safety.

Quince Therapeutics

This California drug developer reported $115 million, keeping the state's biotech pipeline full behind the bigger names.

Based in California. Develops medicines.

Based in California. Develops light-activated cancer drugs.

How the money connects

A fundraising is never just one company. It is a bet that someone else will pay that company, and that someone else's customer will keep paying down the line. Here is that chain for each theme on this page.

The AI chain runs on rented computing

Chip makers sell to clouds, clouds rent to model builders, model builders sell to app makers, and app makers sell to the rest of us. This quarter's filings cluster in the squeezed middle: Baseten, SharonAI, and the chip-link startups Point2, Attotude, and nEye all raised because demand is pressing from both ends. When Generalist's robots or Harvey's lawyers need more computing, that money flows straight back up the chain. That is why infrastructure rounds arrive first and keep arriving.

The defense chain starts with the taxpayer

The Pentagon signs contracts, like Saronic's $392 million ship order, and startups turn those contracts into more fundraising. Then the startups buy parts: Vulcan's magnets go into motors, motors go into drones and ships, and software from Shield AI or Quartermaster tells them where to go. Four separate customers of the U.S. Navy appear in this dataset alone. The reasoning is simple to state and hard to argue with: as long as the contracts keep coming, each link can keep raising.

The money chain takes a cut at every step

Circle issues digital dollars and earns interest on the reserves, but it shares about half of that interest with Coinbase, its biggest distributor. That is the pattern for the whole theme: Ramp and Mercury sit between startups and their spending, Kraken sits between traders and their coins, and each takes a fee. Counterpart, Strala, and Gravie do the same for insurance and health plans. When filings rise, the toll collectors raise next, which is exactly what this quarter shows.

The health chain ends with patients

Toolmakers like Cellanome sell to labs, labs run trials improved by Beacon's headbands, and trial results become drugs from Quanta, Lycia, Climb, or Stipple. Devices from Concept and Route 92 go to surgeons, Magnus sells directly to a famous hospital, and Radiology Partners, ECPH clinics, ChartSpan nurses, and Cerebral therapists deliver the care. Verily's $300 million bet is that the most valuable seat is none of these: it wants to be the data layer underneath all of them.

The atoms chain starts in the ground

Mines produce rare earths, Vulcan turns them into magnets, and magnets turn into the motors inside drones, rockets, and electric cars. Launch companies like Stoke and Cowboy then carry other people's equipment — including Spire's trackers and Cesium's radios — into orbit, where Star Catcher wants to beam them power. It is one physical supply chain from a hole in California to a satellite overhead, and this quarter funded nearly every link of it.

Business opportunities

Ten businesses you could start with the products above

Every company on this page sells something. Each idea below buys one of those things and resells it as a service. The pattern is always the same: rent the hard part, own the customer.

01

Rent AI computing to one industry.

Buy computing from Baseten or SharonAI, then sell finished work to architects, video studios, or law firms by the project. They pay for results. You pay by the hour. The gap is the business.

02

Ship niche software with no backend team.

Build on Supabase, guard the code with Socket, and ship packages through Cloudsmith. One developer can run a scheduling or billing tool for a single trade, like dental clinics or gyms, and charge monthly.

03

Offer startup legal packages at scale.

Use Harvey-style legal AI to draft and review routine contracts, then sell fixed-price startup packages: formation, fundraising papers, and hiring documents. Small firms get big-firm output without big-firm headcount.

04

Fly inspection drones for hire.

Buy proven drones in the PDW class and sell roof, farm, and power-line surveys to insurers and utilities. The hardware gets cheaper every year. The inspection report stays billable.

05

Resell satellite data to one trade.

Spire tracks ships and weather from orbit. Repackage that feed for fishing fleets, insurers, or commodity traders who will never buy raw satellite data but will pay for a morning email that tells them what changed.

06

Move money across borders for small exporters.

Use Circle's dollar coin and Kraken's rails to settle invoices for small importers in days instead of weeks. Charge less than the banks and keep the difference. New federal licensing made this legal to build on.

07

Run finance for ten startups at once.

Open their accounts at Mercury, run their spending through Ramp, and place their health plans with Gravie. One fractional finance practice, ten monthly retainers, zero bank branches.

08

Recruit patients for drug trials.

Trials from Beacon, Quanta, and Lycia all need volunteers, and finding them is the slowest part of every trial. A service that matches local clinics to nearby studies gets paid per enrolled patient.

09

Bundle care for one chronic condition.

Copy the ChartSpan playbook: remote check-ins plus a care plan for diabetes or high blood pressure, sold to small employers through plans like Gravie's. Chronic care is recurring revenue by definition.

10

Audit heat safety for outdoor work.

Copy the Heat Safety series model: sell summer safety audits and monitoring to builders, stadiums, and sports leagues, site by site. Hotter summers make this a bigger market every year.
